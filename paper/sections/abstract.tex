\begin{abstract}
Looped language models apply a shared stack of layers several times per token, so one
checkpoint can trade inference compute for accuracy by changing its loop count. In practice the
loop count is fixed per deployment, or chosen per token by a halting gate whose savings do not
survive batched serving. We study how a looped model should allocate depth in multi-step tool
use, where each call is executed and the final answer can be checked. With Ouro-2.6B fine-tuned
for chained calls and evaluated on NESTful, and Dory-1.0-4B as a second model for the
per-step analysis, we find three things. First, choosing the loop count separately for each tool
call appears to help, but a sampling control with the same budget matches it on both models: the
gain comes from search, not from allocating depth. Second, one loop is too shallow and three
loops are no better than randomly mixing two and four at equal compute, so the decision that
matters is two loops or four. Third, how much depth a request needs is a stable property that
the request text does not reveal, but that early loops do: they agree on boilerplate and
disagree on the decision tokens of the first tool call. An escalation router that starts every
request at two loops and reads these signals \pending{matches four-loop accuracy at X\% of its
compute} on held-out NESTful requests. We also find that most MathQA validation problems have
a near-duplicate in the training split, which inflates results on this widely used source.
\end{abstract}
